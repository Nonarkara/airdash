# 9. Historical Episodes and What Each One Taught

AirDash was not designed against hypothetical air. Three episode families — Bangkok's winter smog, the North's burning-season crises, and the South's imported haze — define the problem space, and each one contributed a design decision.

## 9.1 Bangkok, January–February 2019: the capital wakes up

In the cool weeks of early 2019, stagnant air held Bangkok's PM2.5 above the standard day after day. The episode peaked with the city ordering more than 400 schools closed, cloud-seeding flights attempted, and trucks spraying water into the air — a gesture experts noted did next to nothing for fine particles. The public reaction reshaped Thai air politics: masks sold out, PM2.5 entered everyday vocabulary, and the government eventually declared dust a national-agenda problem. **The lesson for AirDash:** a capital-scale audience will arrive all at once, mid-episode, with no patience for jargon — the front page must answer "what do I do?" before it explains anything else. And honesty matters: a dashboard must be willing to say that spraying water into the air is theatre while forecast rain is not.

## 9.2 Chiang Mai, 2019 and 2023: the world's worst air, on schedule

In the burning seasons of 2019 and again in March 2023, Chiang Mai repeatedly topped global rankings of the world's most polluted major city, with PM2.5 readings reaching several times the Thai 24-hour standard. Hospitals reported surges in respiratory visits; the haze sat in the valley for weeks because the inversion-capped basin gave it nowhere to go. The most important observation is that none of this was surprising — the same weeks, the same mechanism, nearly every year. **The lesson for AirDash:** seasonal predictability is actionable. The dust-season override, the stagnation component, and the operator checklists all exist because the North's crisis is an appointment, not an ambush.

## 9.3 The South, September–October 2015: haze from abroad

In late 2015, smoke from Indonesian peatland fires — a strong El Niño year — drifted north across the peninsula. Southern provinces that never burn their own fields watched visibility collapse; flights were disrupted and schools closed in the worst-hit areas. **The lesson for AirDash:** local emission controls cannot explain or fix everything. The dashboard carries the ENSO context chip and transboundary framing because sometimes the correct answer to "who is burning?" is "no one within a thousand kilometres of you."

## 9.4 The common thread

Every episode ends the same way: the wind changes or the rain comes. Human measures protect people through an episode; only meteorology ends one. That asymmetry is the entire case for the Rain-Washout engine — if relief comes from the sky, then forecasting relief is as valuable as forecasting harm — and for the confidence intervals, because every one of these episodes also produced sensor outages, saturated readings, and disputed numbers at exactly the moments trust mattered most.
