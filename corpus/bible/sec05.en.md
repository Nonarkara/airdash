# 5. Dust Seasonality: The December–April Window

Thailand's PM2.5 problem has a calendar. AirDash defines the dust season as **1 December to 30 April**, and its national display logic, its verdicts, and its historical comparisons are all built on that window. This chapter explains where the window comes from and why the worst weeks cluster late in it.

## 5.1 Why the season exists at all

During the cool season the northeast monsoon brings dry, subsiding air over the country. Nights are cool and clear, so the ground radiates heat away and a **temperature inversion** forms: a lid of warmer air above colder surface air. Smoke and exhaust emitted under the lid cannot rise past it, and with the rains gone there is no washout either. The same emissions that disperse harmlessly in the wet season accumulate day after day. Bad air in Thailand is therefore rarely a story about a sudden new source — it is a story about the atmosphere's ability to take out the garbage being switched off for months.

## 5.2 The burning season within the season

From roughly February to April the northern provinces add a second driver: open burning. Rice straw and maize stubble are burned to clear fields, sugarcane is burned before harvest, and forest fires — many set for foraging access — sweep the dry hillsides. The North's basin topography makes it worse: cities like Chiang Mai sit in valleys where the inversion acts as a bowl lid. This is why the same weeks return to the top of the world's pollution rankings almost every year, and why AirDash's trend and stagnation components matter most in these months.

## 5.3 Transboundary haze

Fires do not respect borders. Satellite hotspot maps in March routinely show dense fire activity across Myanmar and Laos upwind of northern Thailand, and regional-scale haze rides the prevailing flow into Thai provinces regardless of local burning bans. A separate southern variant exists: in some years, notably 2015, Indonesian peatland fires sent haze north across the peninsula in September and October — a reminder that the "dust season" window describes the North and Center best, and the South marches to a different drum.

## 5.4 El Niño, La Niña, and how hard the season hits

The ENSO ocean state modulates the season's severity. **El Niño** years tend to be hotter and drier over Southeast Asia — less rain means less washout, drier fuels burn more readily, and the season bites harder. **La Niña** years tilt wetter and usually soften it. AirDash ingests the NOAA Oceanic Niño Index every 12 hours and shows it as context: a prior, never a prediction. A strong El Niño reading in November is a reason to prepare filters and policies early; it is not a forecast of any particular bad day.
